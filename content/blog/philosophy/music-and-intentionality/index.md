title: Music and intentionality
date: '2026-06-26T14:50:13.063Z'
---

I recently listened to [an Alex O'Connor podcast](https://www.alexoconnor.com/p/suno-ai-music-generation-and-the) in which he and guest Adam Neely discussed
the impact "AI"<sup>1</sup> is having on music. They covered a range of arenas like whether this portends the end professional musicians (spoiler: no), training generative models consumes inhuman amounts of data thus opening ethical debates around compensation and fair use, and more. Check the podcast out, it is worth listening to. However, while I found
their conversation engaging it was, ultimately, unsatisfying. Unsatisfying in regards to the _relationship_ between AI and music. And I think it is a doozy. So, here's my take.

I was tempted to generalise to AI-and-it's-relationship-to-art but I'd rather hash that out in another little post (sarcasm, thou bewitcheth me again!).
So for the purpose of _this_ little post I'm focussing on music and AI, but I would like to say that music is a medium often used in the expression of art and so relates
to art, but **not** all music is art. Therein lies the dragon that must be slain! But I don't want to jump too far ahead. Walk with me.

Music is, in the most crudest definition I can muster, sounds arranged to elicit a response in a listener.
If there is no listener, there is no music. What kind of response? Anything from embodied (dance) to inward, self-reflective responses.
In some cases a rotten tomato may be invovled. Sufficed to say the range of responses is extremely broad and some may be ethically charged. Consider a piece of music by Mahavishnu Orchestra: they fuse the worlds of jazz and rock. Bringing forth not only the unique voice demanded by jazz adherents but also the virtuistic musicanship demanded by progressive forms of rock. If you are a fan of jazz fusion and listened to a song by this group only to later learn the song was generated by AI, no one ever played those instruments, that would elicit an ethical judgement that would change how you respond to the song for the worse. You are likely to reject it entirely because it violates the ethic that such a listener values. Adam Neely made an [excellent video on this](https://youtu.be/R1QEV9euGAg?si=Pwt11htc69i13ztw).

Now this line of thinking is somewhat easy to dismiss, as Alex O'Connor might do. This experience is loaded with context: (1) the listener is aware of the relevant _ethical_ dimensions (2) you are in a position to learn that the song was generated. It's quite possible you'd hear that song and never learn it was AI-generated and so enjoy it unsullied by ethical judgements. Or it is possible that you just don't care about the musicianship and so can enjoy the song without any of that context!

The rationale is something like: the means by which sounds are arranged, in most cases, does not matter for eliciting the _intended_ response. And in most cases, people listening to music have no idea how the music came to be before AI anyway, so, generated or not, the lay-listener does not bring ethical judgement to bear and is free to enjoy the music _as presented_.

This line of reasoning is compelling on the surface, but has cracks upon closer inspection. Let's start with the obvious one: is it actually true that people do not care whether music is generated or not? Is it like a pair of shoes? We go into a shoe shop and choose what we like, we don't care whether a human shoe-maker (or even human designer) was involved in producing the shoe if it fits comfortably and looks cool. Can we apply that line of thinking as-is to music? I think the answer is both yes and no.

"Yes": the music industry of the late 20th century has proven that music _can_ be commoditized. It's a good with supply and demand and so a price point can be established. Or rather, it _can_ be a good. A good in the same way sex or paintings can be goods.

"No": music, when art, is ultimately about communication. This is not a commodity. Communcation can be self-communication, but a self-relation is still a relation. This communication bears with it the unique stamp of intentionality. Intentionality is not a commodity<sup>2</sup>.


Looking a bit closer again at how music is made by humans: the more music is suffused with intentionality the deeper it can resonate. Now, what does that mean? You can generate music using an LLM, but the possibility to layering your intentions into arranging each sound is completely abstracted (and so removed) from you.

Therefore, I would say: music created by humans and music generated by AI can be equally shallow with respect to _intentionality_ but humans are uniquely positioned to compose music suffused with intentionality and so _deepen_ the possibility of resonance -- that aspect of a piece of music which keeps it alive for decades, if not generations.


## Notes

1. AI here referring to generative LLMs in making music à la Suno
2. Another issue with thinking that ethics in music do not matter is the relationship between virtue, character and ethics. As in many worthwhile human pursuits, musicians ahere emerse in an ethical framework for the affect it will have on their character. For them to become "more fully human". This is an idea Jonathan Pageau talked about with respect to [humanity and technology](https://youtu.be/yZUuKzDQSsI?si=y7wpyG4XBx9gerqi).
