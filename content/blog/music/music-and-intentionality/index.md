title: Music and intentionality
date: '2026-06-26T14:50:13.063Z'
---

I recently listened to [an Alex O'Connor podcast](https://www.alexoconnor.com/p/suno-ai-music-generation-and-the) in which he and guest Adam Neely discussed
the impact "AI"<sup>1</sup> is having on music. They covered a range of arenas like whether this portends the end professional musicians (spoiler: no), training generative models consumes inhuman amounts of data thus opening ethical debates around compensation and fair use, and more. Check the podcast out, it is worth listening to. However, while I found
their conversation engaging it was, ultimately, unsatisfying. Unsatisfying in regards to the _relationship_ between AI and music. And I think it is a doozy. So, here's my take.

I was tempted to generalise to AI-and-it's-relationship-to-art but I'd rather hash that out in another little post (sarcasm, thou bewitcheth me again!).
So for the purpose of _this_ little post I'm focussing on music and AI, but I would like to say that music is a medium often used in the expression of art and so relates
to art, but **not** all music is art. Therein lies the dragon that must be slain! But I don't want to jump too far ahead. Walk with me.

Music, in the crudest definition I can muster, is sounds arranged to elicit a response in a listener.
If there is no listener, there is no music. What kind of response? Anything from embodied (dance) to inward, self-reflective responses.
In some cases a rotten tomato may be invovled. Sufficed to say the range of responses is extremely broad and some may be ethically charged. Consider a piece of music by Mahavishnu Orchestra: they fuse the worlds of jazz and rock. Bringing forth not only the unique voice demanded by jazz adherents but also the virtuistic musicanship demanded by progressive forms of rock. If you are a fan of jazz fusion and listened to a song by this group only to later learn the song was generated by AI, no one ever played those instruments, that would elicit an ethical judgement that would change how you respond to the song for the worse. You are likely to reject it entirely because it violates the ethic that such a listener values. Adam Neely made an [excellent video on this](https://youtu.be/R1QEV9euGAg?si=Pwt11htc69i13ztw).

Now, this line of thinking is somewhat easy to dismiss outside of these listeners. O'Connor challenged Neely along these very lines. The picture above is loaded with context that does not broadly hold for any number of possibilities: (1) the listener is not aware of the relevant ethical dimensions (2) without someone telling you, you may not know the song was generated (3) despite knowing the song was generated, it still elicits a response you value, so you don't chuck it out just because some music nerd said it's an abhorration, we'd be shot of a lot of pre-AI music this way!

The rationale is something like: the _means_ by which sounds are arranged, in most cases, does not matter for eliciting a response. And in most cases, people listening to music have no idea how the music came to be before AI anyway, so, generated or not, the lay-listener does not bring ethical judgement to bear and is free to enjoy the music _as presented_.

This line of reasoning is compelling on the surface, but has cracks upon closer inspection. Let's start with the obvious one: is it actually true that people do not care whether music is generated or not? Is it like a pair of shoes? We go into a shoe shop and choose what we like, we don't care whether a human shoe-maker (or even human designer) was involved in producing the shoe if it fits comfortably and looks cool. Can we apply that line of thinking as-is to music? I think the answer is both yes and no.

"Yes": the music industry of the late 20th century has proven that music _can_ be commoditized. It's a good with supply and demand and so a price point can be established. Or rather, it _can_ be a good. A good in the same way sex or paintings can be goods. In [this video](https://youtu.be/sjJrR1OdAIg?si=gy859FP8cAg-iy9U) Rick Beato talks about the "mechanization" of music production. While not the point of the video, he outlines what essentially is the dialling in of fodder (data) for the training of generative models. AI music tends to the middle-of-the-road. It sounds like almost any number of other songs you have already heard in that genre... It's repeating variations on "successful" formulas (and I mean formula in the most technical term possible) regurgitated a number of times approaching infinity. And guess what, people have been doing that for decades, long before AI! Because it works, sort of!

"No": music, when art, is ultimately about communication. Successful communication resonates and creates a response<sup>2</sup>. If you ask me: does it matter that the sender of this communication _intended_ to send _this_ communcation? The answer is an emphatic _yes_. It affects what I would do with the message. And so we have departed the realm of commodity. This communication bears with it the unique stamp of intentionality. Intentionality is not a commodity<sup>3</sup> and is does not roll off an assembly line.

Looking a bit closer again at how art is made in music, this is my central thesis: the more music is suffused with intentionality the deeper it can resonate, the more "human" and the more musical, artistic and interesting it becomes. Let's put a pin in that for a second.

It is category mistake liken what it's like to be a human and what it's like to be a generative model. The structure and mark of human intentionality is uniquely, well, human. That is because it is embedded in the very structure of our conciousness, the being that we are. Put differently: you cannot separate the _stuff_ from what it _does_. Silicon-based pattern matching is not the same as the pattern matching embodied humans do and it probably never will be, and that's fine! Silicone can imitate humans in music making, but it's fundamentally doing a different kind of thing. This does not mean it is of no value, wrong or bad. It can be great, even when applied in music!

I guess the point I'm trying to make runs a little deeper: AI and humans can produce music of more or less the same quality: both can elicit responses in listeners and both can be equally _shallow_ in their intentionality. The kicker is that because generating music via, for example, Suno is a wholistic prompt-in, song-out process the intentionality suffused in the music remains low. This is not about _effort_, but _intentionality_. A given Jackson Pollock may be considered a low-effort painting, but there is no denying that it was immensly impactful because of the intention of the artist. This is what resonated! Some people hated it, others loved it! In my opinion this holds with music as a medium as well and even more so as the music we listen to bookmarks moments and seasons of our lives.

If anything AI-music has underscored the following questions for musicians on the lifelong quest of making music: what are _you_ trying _say_?
