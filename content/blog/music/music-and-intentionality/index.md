---
title: Music and intentionality
date: '2026-11-12T15:59:13.063Z'
---

I recently listened to [an Alex O'Connor podcast](https://www.alexoconnor.com/p/suno-ai-music-generation-and-the) in which he and Adam Neely sparred over what AI<sup>1</sup> means for music. They talked about compensation, fair use, and whether AI portends the end of professional musicians. Their conversation left me with a question, though. When a song moves me, why does it matter who made it and what they were trying to say? So, here's my take.

I think a lot of that connection comes down to _intentionality_. By that I mean how much of what the artist is trying to say comes through in the music. It's the antidote to what I'll call "shallow music". Music can be art to different degrees. But I don't want to jump too far ahead. Walk with me.

Music is sound arranged to elicit a response in a listener. If there is no listener, there is no music. So in some real way music is a relation. A sender and a receiver. You might dance, become reflective, or think differently about the person who made it. On occasion, a rotten tomato may be involved.

The splatters in a Jackson Pollock can look random. If I told you I'd knocked over some paint cans and got the same giant mess by chance, would you look at it the same way? The paint would be in all the same places. What changes is what you know about how it got there.

<img src="../../pics/music-and-intentionality/1A-oil-enamel-canvas-Jackson-Pollock-Museum-1948-2248068391.jpg" alt="A given Jackson Pollock" title="JP">

Take Mahavishnu Orchestra. They bring together the individual voice prized in jazz and the virtuosity of progressive rock. You need to hone your craft in this genre. Imagine hearing what you think is one of their songs, then finding out AI generated it and no one ever played those instruments. If part of what you loved was hearing skilled players perform incredible music, you might feel cheated. Adam Neely made an [excellent video taking this idea further](https://youtu.be/R1QEV9euGAg?si=Pwt11htc69i13ztw).

O'Connor pushed back: a song can move you before you know how it was made. It might still move you after you find out. You don't have to chuck it out just because some music nerd says it's an abomination (we'd be shot of a lot of music made before AI this way).

So... Is music more like a pair of shoes? We go into a shoe shop and pick what we like. If the shoes fit and look cool, do we care who made them or how? 

The music industry of the late 20th century showed that music _can_ be a commodity. In [this video](https://youtu.be/sjJrR1OdAIg?si=gy859FP8cAg-iy9U), Rick Beato talks about the "mechanization" of music production. It got me thinking about what happens when music made to a formula becomes training data. A lot of AI music sounds like a dozen songs you've already heard in that genre, familiar formulas regurgitated a number of times approaching infinity. And guess what, people have been doing that for decades, long before AI! Because it works and makes money.

Here's where I'd push back: I think you can treat music like shoes up to a point. When music is art, I think it's about communication.<sup>2</sup> Does it matter to me that someone _meant_ to say _this_ through a piece of music? An emphatic _yes_. It affects what I do with the message. Keep or dismiss. The artist's intention gives the music its particular stamp.

Even more than this: the more a piece is _suffused_ with an artist's intention, the more deeply it can resonate in this way. It can bring back a memory, let you share in someone else's feelings, or help you make sense of your own. That's part of what makes music feel like art to me. There is a relationship between depth and intentionality. Intention is ultimately what makes music _soulful_.

I think it's a category mistake to treat what a model does as a kind of human experience. A model can imitate the sounds we make, often to great effect. The intention a musician brings to a piece comes out of living in a body, moving through the world, and having a relationship with oneself. When we try to communicate, we look for someone who can recognise something of their own experience in ours.

Music made with AI _can_ be good music. Humans can also make lazy, uninspired, un-**intentional** music that many people enjoy. You can get an echo of an echo of an echo either way... Effort and intentionality are two different things. The kicker with tools like Suno is how many musical choices you leave on the table. Prompt in, song out. What if Suno plays a smaller role in bringing a composition together? I'd ask the same question. Which choices did the musician make, and how much of what you are hearing is a direct product of their intention? Pollock chose how to move and let the paint fall where it fell. There is room for randomness, but what is its relationship to intent?

If anything, AI music has brought me back to a question for myself and my fellow musicians. On your lifelong quest to make music, what are _you_ trying to say?

## Notes

1. Here, AI means tools that generate music, à la Suno.
2. Communication can be self-communication, but a self-relation is still a relation. I think some old Danish guy said something like that.
